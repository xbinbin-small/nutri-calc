"It's in the fridge — reheat it tomorrow and it's fine." That's the default judgment many households make about leftovers. But the fridge isn't a safe box; it only slows microbial growth and can't keep food safe forever. Whether a dish is still good the next day depends on how long it sat out, whether it was contaminated, whether the fridge is cold enough, and whether reheating is thorough — not simply on "did it go in the fridge."

This article is for people who cook at home and deal with leftovers regularly, to help you keep the risk lower.

## The bottom line first

- The fridge only slows bacterial growth; it doesn't kill bacteria or keep food "fresh forever."
- Refrigerate leftovers promptly; don't leave them at room temperature for long.
- Reheat thoroughly; don't reheat the same dish repeatedly or move it in and out of the fridge over and over.
- Infants, pregnant women, older adults and people with weak immunity should be especially cautious with leftovers.
- If it smells off, has changed color, is slimy, or has unknown origin/time, better to discard it.

## Don't let food "sit out" too long

Bacteria multiply fast at room temperature. Leaving cooked food on the table or in the pot to cool slowly gives bacteria time to grow. A safer approach:

- After eating, promptly portion and refrigerate what you want to keep;
- Store raw and cooked foods separately to avoid cross-contamination;
- Don't leave a big pot to cool slowly on its own — dividing it into shallower containers cools it faster.

## Reheating: thorough, and not repeated

The key to reheating leftovers is "heated through" — soups, meat, poultry, eggs and seafood should all be fully heated, not eaten just because the surface is warm. Also aim to "heat only what you'll eat," avoiding taking one dish out, reheating it and putting it back repeatedly; each extra round adds a chance for contamination and spoilage.

## Who should be more careful

Infants, pregnant women, older adults and people with weakened immunity are more vulnerable to foodborne illness and should be more cautious with leftovers — ideally eating freshly cooked food.

If several people who ate together develop vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain or fever, seek care promptly and keep the suspect food or packaging for professionals to assess.

## Summary

The core of leftover safety isn't "is it in the fridge," but: refrigerate promptly, separate raw and cooked, reheat thoroughly, and don't reheat repeatedly. When in doubt, when it's been too long, or when it already looks or smells wrong, don't cling to it — compared with your health, a plate of leftovers is cheap.
