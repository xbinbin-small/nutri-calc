A leg cramp in the middle of the night, and many people immediately conclude: "Must be calcium deficiency — time to buy calcium pills." But it's not that simple. Leg cramps, back aches and bad teeth are not "exclusive signals" of calcium deficiency — overexertion, cold, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, certain medications, and nerve or vascular problems can all cause similar discomfort.

Blaming every discomfort on "low calcium" and then blindly loading up on calcium pills may both miss the real issue and add new risks. This article makes clear how to get calcium, and what besides calcium matters.

## Cramps aren't necessarily low calcium

Occasional leg cramps are common, with many possible causes. If cramps are persistent or frequent, or come with marked pain, weakness or numbness, see a doctor first to find the actual cause, rather than assuming "calcium" and supplementing it away. Treating a symptom as the only evidence easily points you in the wrong direction.

## Get calcium from food first

More important than an occasional big dose of calcium pills is having calcium-rich foods regularly in your three meals. Good everyday sources include:

- Milk and dairy (milk, yogurt, cheese) — relatively well absorbed;
- Soy and soy products (tofu, dried tofu);
- Small fish and shrimp (eaten with bones/shells);
- Some dark green vegetables (such as Chinese kale and bok choy).

Perimenopausal women, older adults and those at higher fracture risk should, beyond diet, also watch bone density and follow individualized medical advice.

## Calcium doesn't fight alone

Calcium alone doesn't guarantee strong bones. Bone health is a combination:

- **Vitamin D**: helps absorb calcium — from moderate sun, food, or supplements under medical advice;
- **Weight-bearing and resistance activity**: walking, jogging, strength training stimulate bone;
- **Overall nutrition**: protein, vitamin K and others also take part in bone metabolism.

Conversely, blindly taking high-dose calcium long term isn't necessarily beneficial and may add risk, especially for people with kidney stones, hypercalcemia or kidney problems. Whether to take a calcium supplement, and how much, should be based on dietary assessment and medical judgment — not "others take it, so I will too."

## Summary

Don't rush to pin leg cramps on "low calcium." Get calcium first from foods like dairy, soy products, small fish and shrimp, and dark green vegetables, paired with vitamin D, sunlight and weight-bearing activity. Bone health relies on a long-term combination, not on calcium tablets as an all-purpose answer; if symptoms persist, or you're unsure about a supplement, leave the judgment to a doctor.
