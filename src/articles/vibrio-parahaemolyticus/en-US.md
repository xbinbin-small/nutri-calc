As summer arrives, seafood stalls, raw-marinated dishes and chilled clams take center stage again. But every year, once coastal areas hit the hot season, emergency rooms see more food-poisoning cases of "vomiting and diarrhea" — and a very common culprit is **Vibrio parahaemolyticus**. Naturally salt-loving and fond of seafood, it is one of the most frequently seen pathogens in bacterial food poisoning in China.

This article walks you through how it makes people sick, what the symptoms are, and — most importantly — how to keep it out of your kitchen.

## The bottom line first

- Vibrio parahaemolyticus is a salt-loving bacterium widespread in seawater and seafood, and a common cause of bacterial food poisoning.
- The main routes of infection are: raw or undercooked seafood, raw–cooked cross-contamination, and leftovers not thoroughly reheated.
- Typical symptoms are upper abdominal pain, diarrhea (which can show a characteristic "meat-washing-water" bloody stool) and fever.
- Prevention comes down to four things: thorough heating, separating raw and cooked, fully reheating leftovers, and kitchen and utensil hygiene.
- With severe symptoms, seek care promptly and don't self-medicate — and watch especially for dehydration and electrolyte imbalance.

## What it is

Vibrio parahaemolyticus is a **gram-negative bacillus**, often appearing curved, rod-shaped or filamentous under the microscope, and it forms no spores. It carries an important marker of pathogenicity — a **positive Kanagawa test**. Its defining trait is being "salt-loving": it grows well in salty marine environments, so sea fish, shrimp, crab, shellfish and other seafood are its most common hiding places.

## How we get infected

Poisoning from this bacterium is often tied to a few habits in everyday eating and kitchen work:

- **Raw or undercooked seafood**: the most direct route. Bacteria on seafood (and sometimes contaminated meat or poultry) get eaten without thorough heating.
- **Cross-contamination in the kitchen**: using the same knife and cutting board for raw seafood and cooked food, or storing raw and cooked together, lets bacteria transfer from raw to cooked. Eating these "re-contaminated" cooked foods causes illness all the same.
- **Mishandled leftovers**: contaminated food left out at room temperature, or refrigerated then eaten without thorough reheating, raises the risk.

## What the symptoms look like

Infection usually brings acute gastrointestinal symptoms, typically:

- Upper abdominal pain and fever;
- Frequent diarrhea, sometimes with a characteristic "meat-washing-water" bloody stool.

Note that these symptoms can be confused with dysentery caused by Shigella, or with other seafood poisonings. So if you develop clear abdominal pain, bloody stool, persistent vomiting or fever, **don't take medicine on your own by guesswork** — seek care promptly and let a doctor make the differential diagnosis.

## How to prevent it: guard the "entrance" and the "kitchen"

- **Thorough heating is king**: cook seafood and meat all the way through; don't chase "freshness" by eating raw or semi-raw seafood of unknown origin.
- **Separate raw and cooked, block cross-contamination**: ideally keep two sets of knives and boards, handle and store raw and cooked separately; after touching raw seafood, wash hands and clean surfaces and utensils thoroughly.
- **Handle leftovers properly**: refrigerate leftovers promptly, reheat thoroughly before eating again, and never eat spoiled or expired food.
- **Keep utensils and surroundings clean**: clean knives, boards and cloths regularly so bacteria have no corner to breed in.

## If you do get sick

If you suspect food poisoning, seek care as soon as possible. The doctor may sample stool, leftover food, and processing items such as knives and cutting boards for testing (e.g., bacterial culture, toxin detection) to identify the pathogen.

Management of Vibrio parahaemolyticus is usually decided by a doctor based on the condition, and may include symptomatic care such as reducing fever, easing pain and controlling diarrhea, with antibiotics if needed. A key point is **correcting dehydration and electrolyte imbalance with fluids** — when diarrhea and vomiting lose large amounts of water and electrolytes, rehydration is often more important than "stopping the diarrhea." What medicines to use, and whether antibiotics are needed, should be a doctor's judgment, not something to buy and take on your own.

## Summary

Vibrio parahaemolyticus may sound fierce, but its weaknesses are clear: it can't survive thorough heating or a properly clean kitchen. Cook food through, separate raw and cooked, fully reheat leftovers, and keep utensils clean, and you keep this "seafood killer" off your table. If you do get unlucky, seek care promptly and focus on rehydration — don't tough it out and don't self-medicate — and you'll get through it more smoothly.
