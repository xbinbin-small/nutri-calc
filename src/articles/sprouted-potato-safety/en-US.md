When potatoes sit too long and sprout, many people wonder: "If I dig out the sprout, is it safe to eat?" Tossing them feels wasteful, but eating them feels risky. Behind that hesitation is a food-safety issue: sprouting, greening or a bitter taste often means the natural toxins in potatoes — glycoalkaloids (commonly called solanine) — have risen, concentrated especially in the sprouts, eyes, skin and greened areas.

This article helps you judge when it's safe to eat after trimming, when to discard decisively, and how to avoid the problem at the source.

## What sprouting, greening and bitterness mean

Glycoalkaloids are substances potatoes produce to protect themselves; at normal levels they're low and harmless to people. But when potatoes sprout, turn green from light exposure, or are stored poorly, the levels rise. Eating too much can cause poisoning symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea and dizziness. A bitter taste is often a signal that these substances have increased.

## When you can eat it, and when to toss it

A simple principle to judge by:

- **Only tiny sprout buds, no greening or off smell**: you can thoroughly dig out the sprouts and eyes, cut away a generous margin of surrounding skin and tissue, then cook it well before eating.
- **Obvious sprouting, large greened areas, bitter taste, gone soft/shriveled or spoiled**: don't be reluctant — discard it. Here, "cutting off the sprout" is unreliable, because the toxin may have spread.

## Heating is not a cure-all

Don't count on "cook it and it's fine." Glycoalkaloids are fairly heat-stable, and ordinary cooking can't reliably destroy them. So handling sprouted potatoes is about "selecting" and "discarding," not "boiling."

Prevention beats remedy:

- Don't buy too many at once — buy what you'll eat;
- Store in a cool, dark, dry place, away from heat and damp that trigger sprouting;
- Check regularly and deal with sprouting or greening early.

## If you eat some and feel unwell

If, shortly after eating, you develop clear nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain or dizziness, seek care promptly and tell the doctor what you ate. Children, older adults and pregnant women should be especially cautious.

## Summary

For sprouted potatoes, remember three things: small sprouts can be dug out, large greened or bitter ones should be tossed, and heating is unreliable. The truly worry-free approach is buying small amounts, storing away from light, and eating them soon — cutting the chance of sprouting and spoilage at the source.
