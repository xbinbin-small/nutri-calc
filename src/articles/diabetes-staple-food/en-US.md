When people with diabetes hear "manage your blood sugar," many immediately think they should eat less rice — or skip rice, noodles and steamed buns entirely. That's the wrong direction. Staple foods are indeed a major driver of post-meal blood sugar, but the goal of diabetes nutrition is not to drive carbohydrates to zero. It is to keep total energy appropriate while making your staples measured in amount, better in type, and steadier in combination — then keep adjusting based on your glucose readings.

This article is for adults with type 2 diabetes, people with prediabetes, and whoever does the cooking at home. If you have type 1 diabetes, gestational diabetes, kidney disease, or are using insulin secretagogues or insulin, make individualized changes under the guidance of your doctor or a nutrition professional.

## The bottom line first

- People with diabetes can eat staple foods — the point is to control the total amount.
- Prefer whole grains, mixed beans and tubers, which tend to produce a steadier glucose response.
- For adults with diabetes, carbohydrates are often suggested to supply about 45%–60% of total energy.
- Low-GI staples such as whole grains and mixed beans are recommended to make up at least one third of your staples.
- You can change your eating order: vegetables and protein foods first, staples last.
- Glucose responses vary between people — even the same food should be judged against your own monitoring.

## Why staple foods affect blood sugar

Staple foods mainly provide carbohydrates. Rice, noodles, steamed buns, congee, potatoes, sweet potatoes, corn, oats, mixed beans and so on are broken down to varying degrees into glucose, which then affects post-meal blood sugar.

But different staples differ in how fast and how high they raise blood sugar. Refined white rice and flour are highly processed and low in fiber, so they tend to raise post-meal glucose faster; whole grains, mixed beans and some tubers contain more fiber, vitamins and minerals, are digested and absorbed somewhat more slowly, and are more favorable for stable post-meal glucose.

So the question is not "staples or no staples," but how much, which kind, and how to combine them.

## Step 1: Fix your portions before anything else

For people with diabetes, the two worst extremes are: eating a lot at one meal and seeing blood sugar spike afterward; then skipping the next meal entirely, ending up hungry, at risk of hypoglycemia, or overeating later to compensate.

A safer approach is to keep your daily staple amount relatively fixed and spread it across three meals. China's National Health Commission *Dietary Guidelines for Adults with Diabetes (2023)* notes that carbohydrates may supply 45%–60% of energy for people with diabetes; at an energy level of 1,800–2,000 kcal for an adult, that is roughly 200–300 g of total carbohydrate per day. Note that this refers to "total carbohydrate," not "weight of staple food."

You don't need to count grams from day one. Start with a simple method: keep each meal's staple portion relatively fixed — for example, a small bowl of rice per meal, or an equal swap to oats, mixed-grain rice, sweet potato, corn or mixed-bean rice. Then check your 2-hour post-meal glucose; if it is consistently high, adjust together with your doctor or dietitian.

## Step 2: Swap some refined staples for whole grains and mixed beans

Whole grains and mixed beans are not "magic glucose-lowering foods," but they are usually more suitable than refined rice and flour as part of a glucose-friendly staple. Recommended swap directions include:

| Currently eat | Replace part of it with | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White rice | Brown rice, oats, buckwheat, corn, quinoa, mixed-grain rice | Don't switch everything at once; those with a weak gut should increase gradually |
| White congee | Mixed-bean rice, oat rice, cooked rice with vegetables | Congee is absorbed fast; many see a clear post-meal rise |
| White steamed buns | Whole-wheat buns, mixed-grain buns | Still a staple — count it too |
| Noodles | Buckwheat or whole-wheat noodles, paired with vegetables and protein | Drink less of the thick broth; avoid a big bowl of plain noodles |
| Sweets and pastries | Plain nuts, unsweetened milk, a small amount of low-GI fruit | Snacks can also be high in carbs and fat |

A practical ratio: at least one third of your daily staples should come from low-GI foods such as whole grains and mixed beans. For example, add brown rice, oat grains, red beans or mung beans to a bowl of rice; or have white rice at one meal and switch to corn, sweet potato and mixed-bean rice at another.

## Step 3: Don't fixate on the staple alone — pairing changes post-meal glucose

The same bowl of rice eaten on its own versus together with vegetables, fish/meat/eggs/dairy and soy products can produce different post-meal glucose. Fiber, protein and a moderate amount of fat slow gastric emptying and carbohydrate absorption, flattening the glucose curve.

A meal can follow this idea:

- Half a plate of non-starchy vegetables: leafy greens, gourds and eggplants, mushrooms, and the like.
- A quarter plate of quality protein: fish, poultry, eggs, lean meat, tofu, unsweetened milk, etc.
- A quarter plate of staple food: rice, mixed-grain rice, tubers, corn, noodles, etc.
- Low-oil, low-salt cooking: control daily cooking oil and salt; don't let "I switched to coarse grains" be undone by very oily dishes.

If your post-meal glucose swings a lot, try adjusting your eating order: vegetables first, then protein foods, and staples last. The method is simple, and it helps some people.

## Step 4: Watch out for these "healthy-looking" staples

Some foods sound healthy but don't necessarily help with glucose control.

The first group is grain pastes and grain powders. Once grains are milled into powder, their structure is broken and they may be digested and absorbed faster — not necessarily better for glucose control than intact grains.

The second group is white congee and very soft, overcooked porridge. Congee goes down easily and is easy to overeat; many people with diabetes see a clear post-meal rise after it.

The third group is "sugar-free biscuits" and "coarse-grain biscuits." Sugar-free does not mean carb-free, nor low-calorie. Biscuits often contain flour and fat, and overeating them affects blood sugar and weight all the same.

The fourth group is "healthy foods" like sweet potato, corn and pumpkin. You can eat them, but if you have already eaten sweet potato or corn, reduce other staples such as rice, buns and noodles accordingly — don't add them on top.

## Step 5: Use glucose monitoring to find your own staple tolerance

There is no single fixed menu for diabetes that suits everyone. Age, weight, activity level, medication plan, gut function, and whether you also have kidney or cardiovascular disease all affect how staples should be arranged.

It helps to look at a "food log" and a "glucose log" together. For example, record for three days in a row what you ate for breakfast, how much, and your 2-hour post-meal glucose. You will gradually find that white congee, noodles, rice, mixed-grain rice and sweet potato don't all give you the same response.

If a particular staple consistently spikes your glucose, first try three things: reduce the portion, switch to a more intact grain or to mixed beans, and add more vegetables and protein. If it is still poorly controlled, bring your records to your doctor or dietitian — don't blindly cut out staples on your own.

## A simple one-day staple-food example

This is only a general example, not a prescription:

| Meal | Staple plan | Pairing suggestion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breakfast | Oat rice or a small whole-wheat bun | Egg, unsweetened milk, dressed or lightly stir-fried vegetables |
| Lunch | A small bowl of mixed-grain rice | Fish or chicken, tofu, two kinds of vegetables |
| Dinner | Sweet potato or corn replacing part of the rice | Leafy greens, mushrooms, lean meat or soy products |
| Snack | If needed, a little low-GI fruit, unsweetened yogurt or plain nuts | Whether to snack depends on medication and glucose swings |

If post-dinner glucose runs high, check three things first: whether the staple was excessive, whether there were congee/powder/noodle/pastry foods, and whether you sat for a long time after eating.

## FAQ

### Can people with diabetes eat rice?

Yes, but in measured amounts. Rice is a staple; the key is controlling the portion and pairing it with vegetables and protein foods as much as possible. If your post-meal glucose tends to be high, try swapping some white rice for brown rice, oats or mixed beans, or reduce the amount of rice.

### Is less staple food always better for people with diabetes?

No. Eating too little staple food can lead to hunger, hypoglycemia risk and unbalanced nutrition, and may skew the share of energy from protein and fat. A more sensible approach is to control the total amount and prefer low-GI, high-fiber staples.

### Is congee always off-limits?

Not absolutely, but many people see a fast post-meal rise after congee, especially white-rice congee and very soft, overcooked porridge. If you want it, have a small amount, not too watery or overcooked, paired with egg, soy products and vegetables, and monitor your post-meal glucose.

### Do sweet potato, corn and pumpkin count as staples?

Sweet potato and corn are part of staples or staple substitutes, and pumpkin also contains some carbohydrate. If you eat these, reduce other staples such as rice, noodles and buns — don't add extra just because they are "healthy."

## Summary

How a person with diabetes should eat staple foods comes down not to "not eating" but to "eating wisely." Fix the staple amount first; then swap part of the refined rice and flour for whole grains, mixed beans and tubers; pair meals with plenty of vegetables and quality protein; adjust the eating order when needed; and finally use glucose monitoring to confirm whether this way of eating suits you.

Truly stable glucose-friendly eating doesn't rely on extreme avoidance, but on a long-term, doable diet structure. Starting from your next meal, make one small change: replace a third of your white rice with whole grains or mixed beans, and see what your post-meal glucose tells you.
