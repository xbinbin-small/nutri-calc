Raw-marinated seafood, sashimi, and liquor-soaked shrimp and crab are a beloved "fresh taste" for many. But these raw or semi-raw foods carry the risk of foodborne parasites and bacteria. A common misconception is that strong liquor, vinegar, wasabi or chili can "kill" or "pickle to death" parasites. In reality, these seasonings change the flavor but can't serve as reliable ways to kill parasites or bacteria.

This article isn't urging you to quit entirely — it's helping you see where the risk lies and how to keep it lower.

## Seasonings don't kill parasites

Many people feel a sense of safety from "soaking" and "marinating," assuming liquor and salt solve the problem. But the alcohol concentration, marinating time and seasonings typically used at home are often not enough to reliably kill parasites (such as certain flukes and nematodes) and pathogens. In other words, "marinated" or "dipped in wasabi" does not equal safe.

## Heating is the most reliable home defense

In a home kitchen, **thorough heating** is the most reliable way to lower the risk of foodborne parasites and pathogens. Fully cooking seafood, meat and snails substantially reduces risk. Also keep in mind:

- **Separate raw and cooked**: use different knives, cutting boards and containers for raw and cooked foods to avoid cross-contamination;
- **Proper sources**: choose ingredients with a clear origin and proper storage;
- **High-risk groups, more caution**: pregnant women, infants, older adults, and people with liver disease or weakened immunity should avoid raw foods as much as possible.

## If you feel unwell, don't treat it as an ordinary "upset stomach"

After eating raw or semi-raw seafood, if you develop persistent abdominal pain, diarrhea, fever or vomiting, or have a suspicious raw-food exposure history, seek care promptly and proactively tell the doctor what you ate. Don't casually buy "deworming medicine" on your own, which may delay proper diagnosis and treatment.

## Summary

Pursuing a fresh taste is understandable, but understand this: seasonings can't replace food-safety measures. To eat with more peace of mind, the keys are heating thoroughly, strictly separating raw and cooked, and choosing proper sources; high-risk groups should avoid raw foods. If suspicious symptoms appear, seeking care promptly and honestly sharing your food history beats any "folk remedy."
