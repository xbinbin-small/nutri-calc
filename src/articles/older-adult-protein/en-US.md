Many older adults' day goes like this: a bowl of white congee with pickles for breakfast, and some meat only at lunch and dinner. On the surface "there's meat," but the day's protein is quite uneven — nearly empty in the morning, then concentrated in one big portion at night. As appetite declines and teeth weaken with age, inadequate protein is fairly common in older adults — and it's tied to muscle loss, falls and slow recovery.

Muscle, immunity and wound healing all depend on protein. For older adults, getting enough protein and spreading it evenly matters more than the phrase "eat more meat."

## The bottom line first

- Older adults need steady, adequate protein to counter muscle loss (sarcopenia).
- The key isn't gorging at one meal but spreading protein across all three.
- Vary the sources: rotate eggs, dairy, fish, poultry, lean meat and soy products.
- With poor teeth, choose softer, easier-to-digest preparations rather than living on congee.
- Those with kidney disease, liver disease and similar conditions need individualized protein plans — follow medical advice.

## Spread protein across three meals

The body can't fully use a huge amount of protein eaten at once. Spreading it evenly across three meals better supports muscle. A simple way to improve:

- **Breakfast shouldn't be congee alone**: add an egg, a cup of milk, or soy milk/tofu;
- **Lunch**: a serving of fish, poultry or lean meat, plus soy products;
- **Dinner**: a moderate amount of protein plus vegetables — no need to pile all the meat here.

Adding an egg and a cup of milk at breakfast is usually easier to sustain — and works better — than eating a couple more bites of meat at dinner.

## Poor teeth? Change the preparation, don't give up

Many older adults can't chew meat well and end up living on congee and soft noodles, leaving protein badly short. The fix is softer, easier-to-chew high-protein foods:

- Steamed egg custard, soft-boiled eggs;
- Steamed fish, low-bone fish such as sole;
- Tofu, soft tofu, tofu pudding;
- Minced meat, meat paste, lean meat stewed until tender;
- Milk, yogurt, cheese.

## With underlying conditions, don't copy "eat more protein"

A caution: "eat more protein" is not a slogan that fits every older adult. For chronic kidney disease, dialysis or liver disease, the type and total amount of protein need to be individualized and decided by a doctor or dietitian — don't copy online tips. Steady weight loss, marked fatigue, repeated infections or swallowing difficulty also warrant prompt medical assessment.

## Summary

The goal of nutrition for older adults isn't to pile on more meat, but to make every meal more complete and easier to digest. Spread protein across three meals, vary the sources, soften the preparation, and pair with appropriate activity — that better preserves muscle and strength. With underlying disease, let a doctor set the plan.
