Around the holidays, dry-fried green beans and green beans with pork are home-cooking staples — but every year some people end up in hospital because of them. The cause is usually not spoiled ingredients but undercooking. When green beans (also called string beans or kidney beans) aren't fully cooked, they contain natural compounds such as saponins and lectins (phytohaemagglutinin) that irritate the digestive tract, causing poisoning symptoms like nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhea.

The risk most often arises in the home kitchen: high heat, a big batch, rushing to plate up — so the outside looks done while the inside is still raw.

## A color change doesn't mean fully cooked

Many assume green beans are done once they "turn from bright to deep green and soften," but not necessarily. The key isn't color but whether they're thoroughly cooked through, with no raw beany smell or crunchy raw texture. Especially when stir-frying a big plateful, heat is uneven and it's easy to leave the outside done and the inside raw.

A safer approach:

- For large amounts, **blanch or boil them first, then stir-fry quickly to season** — don't expect a few tosses over high heat to cook them;
- Don't overfill the pan in one go, so heat spreads more evenly;
- Heat thoroughly until completely soft with no raw taste before plating;
- Similar legumes — string beans, broad beans, oil beans — should also never be eaten undercooked.

## If symptoms appear, don't tough it out with "folk remedies"

If, shortly after eating, you develop clear nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain or diarrhea, or several people at the table feel unwell, stop eating immediately and seek care promptly — don't drink something to "settle it." With persistent vomiting, altered consciousness, difficulty breathing, or symptoms in children or older adults, don't delay.

When you seek care, you can mention you ate undercooked green beans, and keep leftover food if possible to help with assessment.

## Summary

The safety rule for green beans is simple: **cook them thoroughly, and don't gamble on "it looks about done."** Blanch then stir-fry for big batches, don't overfill the pan, and heat until completely soft — and you avoid the vast majority of risk. If poisoning symptoms appear, seek care promptly; don't delay or rely on folk remedies.
