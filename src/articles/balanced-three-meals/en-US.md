When people hear "balanced nutrition," they often picture low-oil, low-salt, bland eating, taking vitamins, or counting every meal's calories precisely. These have some value, but none is the core. Real balance means keeping total energy appropriate while arranging grains and tubers, vegetables and fruit, fish/poultry/meat/eggs/dairy, soy and nuts more sensibly — so that carbohydrates, protein, fat, vitamins, minerals and fiber each do their job.

In other words, a sound diet isn't about chasing one "superfood." It's about turning your three daily meals into a stable, varied structure you can keep up long term.

This article is for most healthy adults. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, a child or teenager, an older adult, very physically active, or already have diabetes, kidney disease, gout, gastrointestinal disease, cancer or similar conditions, make individualized changes under the guidance of your doctor or a nutrition professional.

## The bottom line first

- The first step toward balance is food variety — don't eat only a few foods for the long run.
- Each meal can follow the idea of "half a plate of vegetables, a quarter staple, a quarter quality protein."
- Staples are not the enemy; prefer swapping part of your refined rice and flour for whole grains, mixed beans and tubers.
- Take in protein steadily every day, from fish, poultry, eggs, dairy, lean meat, soy and soy products.
- Fat isn't "the less the better"; the key is controlling the total amount and limiting trans fat and excess saturated fat.
- Vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains provide fiber, vitamins, minerals and many phytochemicals.
- Healthy eating depends not only on what you eat, but also on how you cook, how you eat, and whether you sustain it.

## Balance isn't "eating little" — it's "the right structure"

The body needs energy and a range of nutrients. Carbohydrates, fat and protein are the main energy-supplying nutrients; vitamins and minerals, though needed in small amounts, take part in energy metabolism, immunity, blood formation, bone health, nerve function and more; fiber, though not directly digested and absorbed, helps gut health and blood-sugar and blood-lipid management.

So whether a meal is healthy can't be judged by "low calories" alone. A bowl of white congee with pickles may be low in calories but short on protein, fiber, vitamins and minerals; a serving of mixed-grain rice with lightly stir-fried vegetables and fish or tofu may not be lower in calories, yet is closer to a balanced meal.

A sound diet can be understood as three things:

1. A complete range of foods: cover grains and tubers, vegetables and fruit, animal foods, dairy, soy and nuts each day as much as possible.
2. Reasonable proportions: staples, protein foods, vegetables and fruit, and oil/salt/sugar all have limits.
3. Sensible cooking and eating: less deep-frying and heavy seasoning, regular meals, and no bingeing.

## How to build a meal: split the plate into three parts

Ordinary people don't necessarily need to calculate nutrients precisely every day. A more practical method is to get each meal's plate right first.

| Place on the plate | Recommended foods | Main role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Half the plate | Leafy greens, gourds and eggplants, mushrooms, kelp, carrots and other non-starchy vegetables | Provide fiber, potassium, magnesium, vitamins and phytochemicals |
| A quarter of the plate | Rice, noodles, oats, corn, sweet potato, potato, mixed-bean rice and other staples | Provide the main energy and help spare protein |
| A quarter of the plate | Fish, poultry, eggs, lean meat, tofu, dried tofu, milk, yogurt, etc. | Provide quality protein, iron, zinc, calcium, vitamin B12, etc. |

The advantage of this method is that it's intuitive. If the plate is always "mostly staple with a few vegetables," fiber and quality protein tend to fall short; if you go long without staples and rely only on meat, eggs and vegetables, your energy source can become unstable, hunger increases, and even exercise and work performance may suffer.

## Eat staples — but eat them more "intact"

Staples mainly provide carbohydrates. Carbohydrate is an important energy source, supplying about 4 kcal per gram. The issue is not "staples or not," but the type, amount and degree of processing.

Refined rice and flour taste good and digest fast, but lose more fiber, B vitamins and minerals. Whole grains, mixed beans and tubers keep more of their natural structure, are usually more nutrient-dense, and are more filling.

Day to day, you can swap like this:

| Currently eat | Adjust to | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White rice | White rice plus brown rice, oat grains, buckwheat, corn kernels or mixed beans | Add small amounts at first to avoid gut discomfort |
| White steamed buns | Whole-wheat buns, mixed-grain buns | Still a staple — not unlimited |
| White congee | Mixed-grain rice, oat rice, sweet potato or corn | Congee is easy to eat fast and a lot; those with glucose swings should be careful |
| Biscuits and pastries | Plain nuts, unsweetened milk, a little fruit | "Coarse-grain biscuits" can also be high in oil and sugar |

For most adults, the share of energy from carbohydrate can serve as a rough reference range; long-term extreme low-carb is generally not advised. The safer approach is to control the total amount while improving staple quality.

## Keep protein steady — don't rely on a single meal to make it up

Protein is a key component of muscle, skin, blood, enzymes, hormones and antibodies. Adults need a steady protein intake every day; a rough reference is about 0.8–1.2 g per kg of body weight. For example, a healthy adult weighing 60 kg needs roughly 48–72 g of protein per day, depending on age, activity level and health status.

Quality protein doesn't come only from meat. Fish, poultry, eggs, dairy, lean meat and soy products are common sources:

- Fish: provide quality protein; some fish contain more Omega-3 fats.
- Eggs: high-quality protein; the yolk also has vitamin A, vitamin D, lecithin and more.
- Dairy: provides protein and calcium, suitable as a daily calcium source for most people.
- Soy and soy products: such as tofu, soy milk and dried tofu — quality plant protein sources.
- Lean meat and poultry: provide protein, iron, zinc and B vitamins.

It is better to spread protein across the three meals rather than eating a lot at dinner alone. Someone whose breakfast is only white congee, buns and pickles can first add an egg, a cup of milk or a serving of soy products — often more meaningful than simply cutting staples.

## Fat isn't "the less the better" — the key is the source

Fat supplies about 9 kcal per gram, the most energy-dense nutrient. It helps absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, and takes part in building cell membranes and hormone-related substances. Avoiding fat entirely is not sensible; what really needs control is excess fat, unhealthy fat and hidden fat.

In daily eating, keep three points in mind:

1. Eat less deep-fried food, flaky pastries, cream cakes, and repeatedly fried food.
2. Control total cooking oil, prefer vegetable oils, and rotate the types often.
3. Don't eat fatty meat, processed meats, butter and cream in large amounts over the long term.

Many people assume "I don't eat fatty meat, so I'm not eating much fat," but the fat in takeout, barbecue, hot-pot dipping sauces, oily pastries, biscuits and cakes is often underestimated. If you are gaining weight, have abnormal blood lipids or a higher risk of fatty liver, cutting these hidden fats first is usually more realistic than cutting staples entirely.

## Vegetables and fruit aren't decoration — they're a daily foundation

Vegetables and fruit provide vitamin C, folate, potassium, magnesium, fiber and many phytochemicals. The World Health Organization recommends at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables per day for people aged 10 and over, along with adequate fiber.

In practice you can break the goal into something simpler:

- Have vegetables at every meal if possible, especially deeper-colored ones — dark green, orange-yellow, purple-red.
- Have fruit 1–2 times a day, preferring whole fruit over juice.
- Vegetables are not potato, sweet potato, yam or pumpkin — these are higher in starch and closer to staples or staple substitutes.
- Dressed, lightly stir-fried or steamed are all fine; the key is not letting vegetables become "oil-soaked dishes."

Fruit, too, isn't better in unlimited amounts. Fruit contains natural sugar, and eating a lot adds extra energy. People with abnormal blood sugar should choose the type and amount based on glucose monitoring and medical advice.

## Vitamins and minerals — get them from food first

Vitamins and minerals take part in countless body processes. Vitamin A relates to vision and epithelial health, B vitamins to energy metabolism, vitamin C to antioxidation and collagen synthesis, calcium and vitamin D to bone health, and iron, folate and vitamin B12 to blood formation.

But this doesn't mean everyone needs to take a multivitamin long term. For most people, the priority strategy is still food variety:

- Whole grains, mixed beans, lean meat, eggs and dairy provide a range of B vitamins.
- Dark vegetables and orange-yellow produce help supply carotenoids.
- Dairy, soy products, small fish and shrimp, and some leafy greens help with calcium intake.
- Red meat, animal liver and shellfish provide more readily absorbed iron, but animal liver should not be eaten in large amounts frequently.
- Using iodized salt is an important way for ordinary people to get iodine, but people with thyroid disease should follow medical advice.

Supplements suit cases with a clear deficiency risk or medical need — for example, folate before and in early pregnancy, vitamin D insufficiency, or iron-deficiency anemia. Long-term high-dose supplementation without assessment may exceed the tolerable upper intake level and bring risk instead.

## Cooking method decides a meal's "final quality"

The same ingredients can vary widely in nutritional quality depending on the method. A potato steamed can serve as a staple substitute; fried into chips it becomes a high-oil food. Fish steamed keeps a light flavor; braised with heavy oil and salt clearly increases sodium and fat intake.

Preferred cooking directions are:

- Use more steaming, boiling, stewing, braising and quick stir-frying; use less deep-frying, dry-frying and heavy oil pan-roasting.
- Add less salt, soy sauce, oyster sauce, fermented bean paste, bouillon powder and other high-sodium seasonings.
- Measure the oil before cooking, instead of "pouring by feel."
- Trim visible fat and skin from meat, and eat fewer processed meats.
- For takeout, choose combinations with vegetables and protein, less broth and less deep-frying.

The WHO recommends adults consume less than 5 g of salt per day. In reality, much of people's salt comes not from the salt jar but from soy sauce, pickles, ham, convenience foods, snacks and takeout broth. Cutting salt means tackling seasonings and processed foods together.

## Regular meals matter more than a "perfect menu"

Balance also includes meal timing. Long-term skipping of breakfast, careless lunches and dinner bingeing easily cause uneven energy distribution and affect blood sugar, gut status and weight management.

A steadier rhythm is:

- Breakfast with staple and protein, ideally plus a vegetable or fruit.
- Lunch with a range of foods — not just a big bowl of noodles or a single rice bowl.
- Dinner moderate — avoid too late, too oily, too full.
- Snacks aren't mandatory; arrange them only when hungry or with a special need, preferring unsweetened milk, plain nuts and whole fruit.

If you don't have time for elaborate cooking, start with the "smallest change": add egg and milk at breakfast, add a serving of vegetables at lunch, and swap part of your white rice for mixed-grain rice at dinner. Dietary improvement doesn't have to be all at once — small changes you can sustain are more valuable.

## Common misconceptions

### Is going vegetarian always healthier?

Not necessarily. A well-planned vegetarian diet can be very healthy; but if it just means less meat while mainly eating white rice and flour, fried vegetarian foods, sweets and drinks, you can still fall short on quality protein, iron, zinc, vitamin B12, calcium and Omega-3 fats. Strict vegetarians especially should watch nutrients like vitamin B12.

### Does skipping dinner help with weight loss?

In the short term, weight may drop as total energy falls, but many people then eat more at the next meal, or get hungry, tired and less focused. It is better to control the portion and quality of dinner than to crudely cancel a meal long term.

### Can fruit replace vegetables?

Not fully. Both fruit and vegetables matter, but their nutritional profiles differ. Fruit is usually higher in sugar; vegetables, especially dark ones, provide more varied types of fiber, minerals and phytochemicals. The better approach is to eat both.

### Are more coarse grains always better?

No. Whole grains and mixed beans are worth increasing, but suddenly eating too much may cause bloating, diarrhea or indigestion. People with a weak gut, older adults and those with digestive disease especially should increase gradually.

### Can supplements replace meals?

No. Supplements only add certain components; they can't replace the protein, fat, carbohydrate, fiber and complex food structure in whole foods. Unless there is a clear medical need, don't treat supplements as the main means of balanced nutrition.

## A simple one-day example

This is only an example for an ordinary healthy adult, not an individualized prescription:

| Meal | You can eat this | Pairing focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breakfast | Oats or whole-wheat bun + egg + unsweetened milk + a small portion of fruit | Staple, protein and micronutrients all present |
| Lunch | Mixed-grain rice + steamed fish or chicken + tofu + two kinds of vegetables | Staple not excessive, enough protein and vegetables |
| Dinner | A small amount of sweet potato or rice + mushrooms and greens + lean meat or soy products | Light, moderate, not too late or too full |
| Snack | Plain nuts, unsweetened yogurt, whole fruit | Add only if needed; avoid sweet drinks and pastries |

If a day involves a lot of takeout, prioritize two things: add vegetables, and cut deep-fried and heavily seasoned dishes. Don't give up because one meal was "imperfect" — just return to a normal structure at the next meal.

## Summary

Balanced nutrition isn't about making eating complicated, but about making the three meals that repeat every day more sensible. First achieve food variety, then improve staple quality, ensure a steady supply of quality protein, add vegetables and fruit, control oil/salt/sugar and processed foods, and finally lock in these habits with regular meals.

One meal doesn't need to be perfect, but a week's diet structure should be as balanced as possible. More important than chasing a single nutrition trend is eating richly enough, not in excess, not overly processed, and in a way that fits your own rhythm of life — for the long run.
